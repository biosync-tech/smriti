The integrity contract presented in §3 draws on three lines of prior work: a neuroscience-grounded model of memory consolidation, a logical theory of belief revision, and a recent technique for detecting unsupported claims by structural overlap rather than by LLM judgment. We summarize each here at the level of detail required to follow the contract's formal definitions, then briefly position Smriti's retrieval architecture against existing graph-augmented approaches to clarify what *is not* a contribution of this paper.

## 2.1 Complementary Learning Systems

McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly~\cite{cls-1995} introduced *Complementary Learning Systems* (CLS) to resolve a long-standing tension in connectionist models of memory: a single network cannot simultaneously support fast acquisition of arbitrary new information (which favors a high learning rate) and gradual extraction of statistical regularities across many experiences (which favors a low learning rate, to avoid catastrophic interference). The CLS resolution is biologically motivated: the hippocampus rapidly encodes individual *episodes*, while the neocortex slowly extracts *schemas* — abstracted representations capturing what is invariant across many similar episodes. Episodes are *replayed* to neocortex during rest, and only those replayed sufficiently often, in sufficiently varied contexts, leave durable schema-level traces.

Kumaran, Hassabis & McClelland~\cite{cls-2016} update the framework for modern machine-learning settings, arguing that intelligent agents — biological or artificial — need both fast and slow learning systems. Smriti adopts the CLS metaphor as the basis of its memory-consolidation pass: notes that are frequently accessed, structurally central in the graph, and accessed from diverse retrieval contexts accumulate a consolidation score that, above threshold, can promote them into compressed schema representations. Notes scoring below threshold are flagged for human review — never deleted — preserving the audit trail required by ICH E6(R3)~\cite{ich-e6r3}.

CLS-inspired consolidation is a feature of the broader Smriti system; it is *not* the contribution of this paper, and we mention it only because the consolidation events themselves are subject to the integrity contract — every promotion, flag, or archive produces a hash-chained event. A separate paper will describe the consolidation algorithm and evaluate it against memory-recall benchmarks. Here we treat consolidation as a black box that mutates the graph and is therefore covered by I1 (§3.2).

## 2.2 AGM belief revision

The Alchourrón-Gärdenfors-Makinson (AGM) postulates~\cite{agm-graph-revision} characterize rational belief change in formal systems: when a new piece of information conflicts with an existing belief set, the postulates prescribe how to update minimally — preserving as much of the prior belief set as possible while maintaining consistency with the new information. The postulates partition rational responses into three classes: *expansion* (no conflict; add the new belief), *contraction* (remove the conflicting belief), and *revision* (replace the conflicting belief, possibly with a weaker or augmented version).

Smriti's `memory_store` MCP tool exposes four AGM-derived conflict policies as a parameter: `Overwrite` (last-write-wins, the default for backward compatibility), `Reject` (refuse the write if a conflict exists), `VersionAndKeep` (archive the prior value to a `memory_history` table; the new value becomes current; both are queryable), and `Invalidate` (mark the prior value as superseded but retain it in the audit trail). The integrity contract guarantees that whichever policy is chosen, the choice and its consequences are hash-chained — an auditor can determine not only what an agent currently believes but also what it previously believed and *when its beliefs changed*.

This is a write-time enforcement of the AGM postulates rather than a post-hoc reasoning system over them. Smriti does not derive contraction or revision policies automatically; it provides the mechanisms and requires the caller to choose. The choice itself becomes part of the audit trail.

## 2.3 Citation grounding without LLM-as-judge

A central failure mode of LLM-augmented memory systems is *citation hallucination*: an agent produces a claim with an authoritative-looking source attribution, but the cited source either does not exist or does not contain the claimed content~\cite{factum, citation-grounded-code}. Existing detection methods generally fall into two categories: training a classifier on (claim, source) pairs labeled by humans, or using a second LLM as a judge to verify the support relation. Both approaches inherit the unreliability of the underlying language models — including the very models whose outputs they are meant to verify.

The FACTUM framework~\cite{factum} introduces a *mechanistic* alternative: define a *structural overlap score* between a claim span and a cited source span using only normalized surface-level token overlap. The score is deterministic, has no training data dependency, and is fast to compute. FACTUM frames the score as an evaluation metric — a number to display alongside generated text so a human reader can decide whether to trust it.

Smriti's contribution is to take FACTUM's overlap score and *invert its role*: rather than being computed after the fact, it becomes a structural invariant enforced at write time. Claims whose spans fail the overlap check are *rejected* atomically inside the transactional commit path. The system's failure mode shifts from "the agent submits a hallucinated claim that gets flagged for review" to "the agent cannot submit a hallucinated claim at all." The trade-off — that legitimate paraphrases below the threshold are also rejected — is tunable via the threshold parameter τ (§3.3) and evaluated empirically in §6.4.

## 2.4 Graph-augmented retrieval

Several recent systems explore knowledge-graph structure as a substrate for retrieval-augmented generation, in contrast to the dominant flat-vector-index paradigm. Edge~\cite{magma} demonstrates that *typed graph layers* — separating semantic, temporal, and causal edges — reduce token usage by 95% versus flat retrieval on multi-hop QA. The Graph-Based Memory Survey~\cite{memory-survey} compares 30+ memory systems on multi-hop benchmarks and finds that hybrid graph-plus-keyword retrieval consistently outperforms pure vector retrieval. Zep/Graphiti~\cite{zep-graphiti} introduces *bi-temporal edges* — recording both event time and ingestion time — and shows an 18.5% recall improvement on LongMemEval over single-time-axis baselines.

Smriti adopts all three findings as architectural decisions: typed `LinkType` enums (`Semantic`, `Temporal`, `Causal`, `AiSuggested`, etc.), bi-temporal `valid_from`/`valid_until` columns on every edge, and reciprocal-rank-fusion of FTS5 keyword and `sqlite-vec` cosine retrievers in the hybrid search path. None of these are novel contributions; they are well-validated design choices that we re-use. The contribution of this paper, presented in the next section, is not retrieval architecture but the integrity contract that lifts the architecture from *fast* to *auditable*.
